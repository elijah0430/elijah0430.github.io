  1. Your Language Model is Its Own Critic: Reinforcement Learning with Value Estimation from Actor's Internal States

    Yunho Choi*, Jongwon Lim*, Woojin Ahn, Minjae Oh, Jeonghoon Shim, Yohan Jo

    NeurIPS 2026 Poster

    arXiv Project page
    Overview

    Stable RL with verifiable rewards requires both reliable baselines and diverse training prompts. Group-based methods improve baseline estimates by sampling more responses per prompt, but this leaves fewer distinct prompts under a fixed rollout budget. POISE estimates baselines with a lightweight probe of the actor's hidden states and entropy, without a separate LLM-scale critic. Predicting each rollout's baseline from an independent rollout keeps the policy gradient unbiased with two rollouts per prompt. This allows greater prompt diversity and improves average performance and training stability over the evaluated baselines in multi-domain training.

  2. Dual Mechanisms of Value Expression: Intrinsic vs. Prompted Values in Large Language Models

    Jongwook Han*, Jongwon Lim*, Injin Kong, Yohan Jo

    ICML 2026 Regular

    arXiv Project page OpenReview
    Overview

    Prompting an LLM to express a value can produce different behavior from the model expressing that value without instructions. We examine whether the two rely on the same internal mechanisms. We identify shared neurons that causally contribute to both forms of value expression, alongside components with distinct roles. Components specific to intrinsic expression promote response diversity, whereas those specific to prompted expression strengthen instruction compliance, even on tasks unrelated to values.

  3. ArcANE: Do Role-Playing Language Agents Stay in Character at the Right Time?

    Woojung Song, Nalim Kim, Sangjun Song, Chaewon Heo, Jongwon Lim, Yohan Jo

    EMNLP 2026 Main

    arXiv
    Overview

    A role-playing agent should reflect how a character's values, motivations, and relationships change throughout a story. Yet existing benchmarks largely treat characters as fixed personas or test only what they know at a given point. ArcANE tests whether an agent's responses fit the character's development by presenting the same situation at different narrative stages, including situations outside the original story. Across six models, providing a record of the character's development up to the queried stage improves this fidelity over the evaluated narrative-context baselines.

  4. Learning to Retrieve User History and Generate User Profiles for Personalized Persuasiveness Prediction

    Sejun Park, Yoonah Park, Jongwon Lim, Yohan Jo

    ACL 2026 Findings

    arXiv
    Overview

    Predicting whether a message will persuade someone requires knowing which parts of their history matter for that discussion. Retrieving similar posts or building a generic profile may miss this information. We train a query generator to retrieve useful records and a profiler to summarize them, using their effect on a fixed model's view-change predictions as supervision. No ground-truth profiles are needed. On ChangeMyView, this improves prediction across multiple predictor models. Our analyses show that useful records and profile dimensions vary with both the discussion and the predictor.

  1. The Low-Rank Structure of VLA Reinforcement Learning

    Minjae Oh*, Yoonah Park*, Jongwon Lim*, Yohan Jo

    Preprint (under review)

    arXiv
    Overview

    RL improves vision-language-action (VLA) models, but which parameter changes drive these gains remains unclear. Across the flow-based VLAs we study, low-rank RL updates concentrate in timestep modules. These modules depend only on denoising time, not observations or instructions, yet module-replacement experiments show that their updates capture a large share of RL gains. We link the low-rank structure to training on discrete denoising timesteps. Targeting these modules with LoRA outperforms standard configurations, which typically omit them. Steering along their learned shift directions further improves RL-trained policies without additional RL training.

  2. How Do LLMs Change Predictions Under Negation?

    Jongwook Yoon*, Jongwon Lim*, Sungjib Lim, Woojin Cho, Yohan Jo

    Preprint (under review)

    arXiv
    Overview

    Negating a question should exclude the original answer, yet LLMs often repeat it, especially when they were confident in that answer. In the models we analyze, negation suppresses original-answer retrieval and promotes favored candidates within the answer category. However, these preferences do not reliably exclude the original answer, and the correction can be too weak to overturn it. We therefore propose ALiT, which requires larger corrections for more confident original predictions. ALiT reduces repetition while preserving the answer category and avoiding the sharp diversity loss observed with supervised fine-tuning.

  3. How Language Models Compute Negation: A Mechanistic Study of Factual Negation

    Jongwook Yoon, Jongwon Lim, Yohan Jo

    Mech Interp Workshop @ ICML 2026

    OpenReview PDF Poster
    Overview

    When a language model changes its answer under negation, does it alter factual retrieval or only the final prediction? We study Qwen3 and Gemma 3 on prompts they answer correctly before negation and whose predictions change after negation. Negation interacts with relation information early, while later components shift the prediction away from the original answer. This suggests that negation modifies factual retrieval itself, rather than acting only on a completed prediction.

  4. DAHL: Domain-specific Automated Hallucination Evaluation of Long-Form Text through a Benchmark Dataset in Biomedicine

    Jean Seo, Jongwon Lim, Dongjun Jang, Hyopil Shin

    FEVER Workshop @ EMNLP 2024

    arXiv Code
    Overview

    Biomedical benchmarks often focus on multiple-choice questions, but long-form answers can mix accurate and inaccurate claims that a single correctness judgment cannot capture. We introduce DAHL, a benchmark of 8,573 questions generated from research articles across 29 biomedical categories. Its automated evaluation pipeline splits each answer into individual claims and scores their factual accuracy, allowing evaluation of which parts of a long-form answer are correct.
